HW6: The Resume Glow-Up

CSCI-1210 Fall 2026

Take the most boring document ever invented -- the resume -- and turn it into a web page somebody would actually want to scroll through.

This one is the big finale for HTML and CSS fundamentals, so it pulls in pretty much everything we've done: images, links, tables, classes and ids, the box model, and color palettes. What's new this time: semantic elements (<header>, <nav>, <section>, <article>, <figure>, <footer>), pseudo-classes, display, and position. Still no Bootstrap -- every style on your page comes from CSS that you wrote.

1. Download the starter code

Download the starter code off GitHub and unzip it -- this gives you the project folder you'll build everything in for the rest of these steps.

Make sure that you unzip the folder to /hw6 and store it somewhere that makes sense, right next to your hw3, hw4, and hw5 folders.

2. Pick your applicant

Here's the twist: the resume doesn't have to be yours. You've got two options:

Option A — The Real Deal

Build a resume for yourself -- the you of today, or the you of five years from now with the degree and the dream job. If you go this route, you walk away with something you can actually keep updating and send to people. Future-you will be grateful.

Option B — The Character Application

Build a resume for a character applying for a job they are hilariously wrong (or suspiciously perfect) for. Write it completely straight, like they really want this job. Some ideas:

  • Bowser, applying to be a Royal Princess Security Consultant
  • Darth Vader, applying for Director of Human Resources ("strong track record of employee motivation")
  • Gandalf, applying to run fireworks for children's birthday parties
  • The Kool-Aid Man, applying for Structural Engineer
  • Your cat, applying for Head of Household Security (Experience: staring at the door for 9 hours)
  • A Minecraft Creeper, applying for Demolition Foreman

Either way, your applicant needs real content in every section: at least 3 jobs (or job-like things -- quests, campaigns, clubs, volunteering), some education or training, a set of skills, and one section that's all personality (see step 6).

Warning — this goes on the public internet: Your page will live on csci1210.com where anyone can find it. If you go with Option A, do not put your phone number, home address, birthday, or anything else you wouldn't want publicly-accessible. When in doubt, leave it out -- or make something up (e.g., exampleemail@fake.com).

4. Div Detox

Before you write anything new, open hw6.html in VS Code and look at how Dr. Placeholder's page is built. You'll see a lot of this: 

<div class="header">
                <div class="photo">
                <img src="images/headshot.jpg" alt="Dr. Placeholder">
                <div class="caption">Dr. Placeholder, looking employable</div>
                </div>
                ...
                </div>

                <div class="nav"> ... </div>

                <div class="section" id="experience">
                <div class="job"> ... </div>
                <div class="job"> ... </div>
                </div>

                <div class="footer"> ... </div>

This is lovingly referred to as div soup. The browser has no idea which of those boxes is the header, which is navigation, and which is a job -- they're all just "a box." Your job is to fix that:

  1. Predict. Go through the file and write down which semantic element each <div> should be. (The cheat sheet at the bottom of this page will help.) Then predict: once you swap them, will the page look any different in the browser?
  2. Swap. Replace each one with the right semantic element. Keep the class and id attributes exactly as they are for now. Don't forget the closing tags!
  3. Check. Save and refresh. Most of the page looks exactly the same... but something broke. Find it. Then open css/style.css and figure out why that rule stopped working when the others didn't.
  4. Fix & Explain. Fix the broken rule. Then, right above it in css/style.css, write a CSS comment with your prediction, what actually happened, and why -- in your own words.
Bonus question: If the page mostly looked the same before and after, why bother? Name two people (or programs) that can tell the difference between your page and Dr. Placeholder's, and what they do with it. Add your answer to the same comment.

Once you've written your comment, Dr. Placeholder is fired. Replace everything with your applicant. Just keep the comment.

5. Lock in a color palette

Same deal as HW5: pick a real palette with Adobe Color or Paletton before you start writing colors, then check it with the WebAIM Contrast Checker.

Let your palette match your applicant. A Bowser resume in pastel pink is a choice (a funny one, honestly). A "real you" resume for a software job probably wants something calmer than a Bowser-themed page.

Requirement — document your palette:

In the comment block at the top of css/style.css, list the hex codes for your palette (3–5 of them) and where each one gets used, plus the contrast ratio you measured for your main body text against its background. Still 4.5:1 or better, still no exceptions.

6. Build the resume (hw6.html)

Update the page <title> first (nobody wants a browser tab that says "Dr. Placeholder"), then build out each piece of the page:

Requirement — the page structure:
  • A <header> with your applicant's name, a one-line tagline (the job they want, or a motto), and a headshot inside a <figure> with a <figcaption>
  • A <nav> with links that jump to each section on the page (see the cheat sheet on in-page links below), plus at least one external link -- a portfolio, LinkedIn, GitHub, or your character's wiki page
  • At least 4 <section>s, each with a heading and an id your nav can jump to:
    • About -- a short paragraph pitching your applicant
    • Experience -- at least 3 jobs, each one its own <article> with a job title, where, when, and a list of accomplishments
    • Education & Skills -- schools, training, certifications, and what they're good at
    • A wildcard section -- this is where the personality goes. Awards, hobbies, testimonials from coworkers, "Known Weaknesses," a list of references available upon request (from the dragon)
  • One table somewhere that makes sense -- a skill rating chart, a list of courses with grades, a weekly availability schedule, a "me vs. other applicants" comparison. Use <th> for the headings, like in HW5.
  • A <footer> with a contact link (mailto: -- see below) and a copyright line. Leave the validator <script> where it is.
Note — <section> vs. <article>: These two get mixed up constantly. A <section> is a chapter of your page -- a themed chunk with its own heading, like "Experience." An <article> is a self-contained thing that would still make sense if you cut it out and pasted it somewhere else, like one single job. So your Experience section holds three articles, not the other way around. A good test: would it make sense on its own in a news feed? If yes, it's an article.
Tip — you can still use <div>s: Semantic elements didn't make <div> illegal. If you need a box purely for styling -- to group two things so you can put a border around them, say -- a <div> is still the right tool. The rule of thumb: reach for a semantic element first, and use a <div> when none of them actually mean what you're building.

7. Style it (css/style.css)

Now make it look like someone would hire this person. How it looks is completely up to you, but your stylesheet needs to show off the following:

Requirement — the usual suspects:
  • The comment block at the top of style.css, filled in (including your palette and contrast ratio from step 5)
  • Comments explaining what each section of your CSS is for
  • Rules that use element, class, and id selectors -- and at least a few that target your new semantic elements directly (header, nav, article, ...)
  • Only colors from your documented palette
  • A variety of text properties, and at least one background image somewhere on the page
  • Your table styled the HW5 way: border-collapse, padded cells, and <th> cells that look different from <td> cells
Requirement — box model & display:
  • Your resume sits in a centered column: give it a max-width and center it with margin: 0 auto; so it doesn't stretch edge-to-edge on a big monitor
  • Each job <article> is visibly its own box -- some combination of padding, border, margin, and background
  • Change the display of at least two elements from their default. For example: nav links as inline-block so they can take padding like buttons; a skills list where each <li> is an inline-block "chip" instead of a bullet point; an image set to block so you can center it
Requirement — position:
  • The stamp. Put a little "stamp" or badge on your resume using a position: relative parent and a position: absolute child. Hired! across the header, an "Employee of the Month" badge in the corner of a job, an "Available Now" sticker on the headshot, a "Certified Dragon-Free" seal -- whatever fits. Play with the position playground below first.
  • The nav that won't leave. Make your <nav> stay on screen while you scroll, using position: fixed (or position: sticky, if you want to try it -- see the cheat sheet). Then make sure it isn't covering up the top of your content.
Requirement — pseudo-classes:
  • At least three different pseudo-classes somewhere in your sheet
  • One of them must be :hover on something that isn't a link -- a job article that lights up, a skill chip that changes color, a table row that highlights
  • Your links need to look like links, and look different when you hover over them. If you style :link and :visited, remember the order (see the cheat sheet)
  • Use at least one of the "which child is it?" pseudo-classes: :first-child, :last-child, or :nth-child(). Zebra-striping your table rows with tr:nth-child(even) is a classic.
Warning — the fixed nav eats your content: position: fixed pulls an element out of the normal flow, so the rest of the page slides up underneath it like it isn't there. The first thing on your page will end up hiding behind your nav. The fix is to leave room for it: give body some padding-top at least as tall as your nav. Same thing when you jump to a section -- if the heading is hiding under the nav, that's why.

8. The Hiring Committee

Open hw6.html in your browser, then go find a roommate, a friend, or a family member -- anyone who isn't you. Give them 30 seconds with your page and then close the laptop. Ask them:

  • What's this person's name?
  • What job do they want?
  • Name one thing they're good at.
  • How would you contact them?

If they can't answer all four, your page is burying the important stuff. Real recruiters spend about that long on a resume before deciding, so this is genuinely how it works. Then test the boring parts yourself:

  • Click every nav link. Does each one land on the right section, with the heading not hiding under the nav?
  • Hover over everything. Does something respond that isn't a link?
  • Scroll all the way down. Does the nav stick around? Does your stamp stay where it belongs?
  • Resize the window. Does the centered column still hold together?
  • Does your contact link actually open an email?

About the tools you're using

Semantic element cheat sheet
Element What it means On your resume
<header> Intro content for the page (or for a section/article) Name, tagline, headshot
<nav> A block of major navigation links Your jump-to-section links
<section> A themed chunk of the page, usually with its own heading About, Experience, Education, ...
<article> Something self-contained that makes sense on its own One job
<figure> An image (or chart, or code sample) that goes with the content The headshot
<figcaption> The caption for a <figure> -- first or last thing inside it "Bowser, pictured before the incident"
<footer> Closing info for the page (or for a section/article) Contact info, copyright

Out of the box, every one of these acts like a <div> -- a plain block with no styling. The difference is entirely in what they tell the browser, screen readers, and search engines. Full list: w3schools: HTML Semantic Elements.

Linking to a spot on the same page

Give the destination an id, then point a link at it with a # in front. No page name needed: 

<nav>
                        <a href="#experience">Experience</a>
                        </nav>

                        ...

                        <section id="experience">
                        <h2>Experience</h2>
                        ...
                        </section>

Click the link and the browser scrolls straight to the element with that id. (Every "see above" link on these instruction pages works exactly this way.)

For your contact link, mailto: opens the visitor's email app with your address already filled in: <a href="mailto:zxyz99@etsu.edu">Email me</a>

display cheat sheet
Value What it does Default for
block Starts on a new line and stretches as wide as its parent. Respects width, height, and all margin/padding. div, p, h1–h6, ul, li, section, ...
inline Sits in the line of text, only as wide as its content. Ignores width, height, and top/bottom margin. a, span, strong, em, img
inline-block The best of both: sits in a line like inline, but takes width, height, and padding like block. button
none Gone. Not hidden -- removed from the page entirely, and it takes up no space. The <head> and everything in it

If you've ever put padding on a link and watched it overlap the line above instead of pushing it away, that's inline ignoring you. Switch it to inline-block.

position cheat sheet
Value Still in the flow? top / left / etc. measured from...
static Yes Nothing -- offsets are ignored. This is the default.
relative Yes (its old spot stays reserved) Where it would have been
absolute No The nearest ancestor that has a position other than static (or the page, if there isn't one)
fixed No The browser window. It doesn't move when you scroll.
sticky Yes, until you scroll past it Acts relative until it hits the offset you gave it (like top: 0), then sticks there like fixed

The relative-parent / absolute-child combo is the one to remember: position: relative with no offsets doesn't move the parent at all, it just turns it into the "anchor" that its absolute children measure from. (The nav bar at the top of this page is sticky, by the way. Scroll and watch it.)

Pseudo-class cheat sheet

A pseudo-class goes on the end of a selector with a colon, and matches an element only when it's in a certain state or spot:

Pseudo-class Matches when...
a:link The link hasn't been visited yet
a:visited The link has been visited
:hover The mouse is over it -- works on any element, not just links
:active It's being clicked, right this instant
:focus It's selected with the keyboard (Tab key) or clicked into
:first-child / :last-child It's the first / last element inside its parent
:nth-child(even) It's the 2nd, 4th, 6th... element inside its parent. Also takes odd or a number, like :nth-child(3) 
/* every job lifts a little when you hover it */
                        article.job:hover {
                        background-color: #fff3d6;
                        border-color: #b5651d;
                        }

                        /* no extra space below the very last job */
                        article.job:last-child {
                        margin-bottom: 0;
                        }

Link order matters: if you style more than one link state, write them in the order :link, :visited, :hover, :active. They all have the same specificity, so the last one written wins -- get them out of order and your hover effect quietly stops working on visited links. The classic way to remember it: LoVe HAte.

Want to go further?

Everything required above came out of lecture. If you want this to be the resume you actually send people, a few things worth trying:

  • html { scroll-behavior: smooth; } -- your nav links glide to their section instead of teleporting
  • :target -- styles whichever section your nav just jumped to, so you can flash it like the "see above" links on this page
  • transition -- makes your :hover changes fade in instead of snapping
  • border-radius and box-shadow for a softer, more modern look
  • @media print { ... } -- rules that only apply when someone prints your page. Hide the nav with display: none and you've got a printable paper resume for free

Good places to dig: w3schools: CSS Pseudo-classes and MDN: position. Just make sure your page still validates.
