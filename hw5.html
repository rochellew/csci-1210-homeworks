HW5: Stat Sheet Showdown

CSCI-1210 Fall 2026

Settle an argument with data. Build a page that ranks a roster of your choosing in two HTML tables, then style the whole thing yourself with CSS and the box model.

Open-ended again — you pick the roster, the stats, and the look. What's new this time: tables (including merged cells), the box model, and picking a color palette that actually passes an accessibility check. Like HW4, there's no Bootstrap here: every style on your page comes from CSS that you wrote.

1. Download the starter code

Download the starter code off GitHub and unzip it -- this gives you the project folder you'll build everything in for the rest of these steps.

Make sure that you unzip the folder to /hw5 and store it somewhere that makes sense, right next to your hw3 and hw4 folders.

2. Pick your roster

You need a set of things that can be compared -- because the whole point of a table is that every row has the same columns. Pick something you'd actually argue about:

  • Every fast food chicken sandwich within 10 miles of campus
  • Mario Kart characters, Smash fighters, or Pokémon starters
  • Star Wars movies, Zelda games, albums by one artist
  • Dog breeds, dinosaurs, planets, roller coasters, energy drinks
  • The cars you'd buy if money were no object (0–60, MPG, price, cool factor)
  • Your own made-up league of fighters, monsters, or robots

You'll need at least 6 contenders and at least 4 stats for each one. At least one stat should be a number, so there's something to actually rank by.

Note — tables are for data, not layout: Back in the day, people used tables to lay out entire web pages. Don't. A table is for information that naturally belongs in a grid -- a bunch of things that all share the same properties. Your stat sheet qualifies. Your page header does not.

3. What you're given

Your project folder already contains a starter template:

  • hw5.html -- a page with a header, a nav bar, two table sections, and a footer. It includes a small example table to show you the tags in action, plus the autopsy boxes (see step 4).
  • css/style.css -- your stylesheet, already linked from hw5.html. It has the required comment block at the top, the rules for the autopsy boxes, and plenty of room for your own.
  • images/ -- optional this time. If you want a logo, a background image, or little icons in your cells, optimize them in GIMP (same as HW3) and drop them here.

4. Box Model Autopsy

Before you build anything, you're going to figure out how big a box really is. Open hw5.html in the browser and find the two autopsy boxes. Box A is styled in css/style.css like this: 

#box-a {
    width: 300px;
    padding: 20px;
    border: 5px solid #333;
    margin: 30px;
}

Your job:

  1. Predict. Before you touch DevTools, do the arithmetic on paper. How many pixels wide is Box A on screen? How much horizontal space does it take up including its margin? Write both numbers down.
  2. Verify. Right-click Box A → Inspect. In the Computed tab (Chrome/Edge) or the Layout tab (Firefox) there's a diagram of the box model with the real numbers in it. Were you right?
  3. Collapse. Box B sits directly below Box A. Box A has a 30px bottom margin; Box B has a 20px top margin. Predict the gap between them, then measure it by hovering over the boxes in the Inspector. Is it 50px? Why not?
  4. Explain. In css/style.css, right above the #box-a rule, write a CSS comment with your predictions, what the Inspector actually said, and why -- in your own words.
Bonus question: Both boxes have a background color. Does the background fill the padding? The border? The margin? Change #box-a's padding to 60px, then change its margin to 60px, and watch which one pushes the color around. Add your answer to the same comment.

Once you've written your comment, the autopsy boxes are yours -- restyle them into something that fits your page, or delete them. Just keep the comment.

5. Lock in a color palette

This is the step everybody skips and everybody loses points on. Before you write a single color into your CSS, go pick a real palette:

  • Adobe Color -- spin the wheel, or pull a palette straight out of a photo
  • Paletton -- older-looking, but it will show you which of your combinations are actually readable

Then check your text against its background with the WebAIM Contrast Checker. The minimum contrast ratio for normal body text is 4.5:1.

Requirement — document your palette:

In the comment block at the top of css/style.css, list the hex codes for your palette (3–5 of them) and one word on where each one gets used -- e.g. #1b3a5c -- table headings.

Then add one line with the contrast ratio you measured for your main body text against its background, and which tool you measured it with. If it comes in under 4.5:1, go fix your colors -- that's the whole exercise.

Why we care: Roughly 8% of men and 0.5% of women have some form of color vision deficiency -- around 300 million people. Add everybody squinting at a laptop in direct sunlight and low contrast stops being a style choice and starts being the reason people leave your site. "It looks fine on my monitor" is not a design standard.

6. Table 1 — The Stat Sheet

This is the straightforward one: your roster as a grid. Rows are contenders, columns are stats.

Requirement — the stat sheet:
  • At least 6 rows of contenders and at least 4 stat columns
  • A heading row of <th> cells across the top
  • Each contender's name is also a <th> -- it's the heading for its row, not data
  • Real data. Made-up stats for made-up contenders are fine; blank cells and lorem ipsum are not
  • Every row has the same number of cells (count them -- this is the number one way tables break)

7. Table 2 — The Showdown Grid

Now the fun one. This table has to use merged cells -- both colspan and rowspan, at least once each. Pick whichever shape fits your roster:

  • A bracket. Eight contenders, quarterfinals → semifinals → final. Winners advance by spanning rows.
  • A schedule. Time slots down the side, stages or rooms across the top, and events that span two slots or two stages.
  • A hall of fame. Years down the side, grouped under spanned "era" headings, with award categories across the top.
  • A head-to-head grid. Your roster on both axes, a banner heading spanning the whole top, and merged "N/A" blocks down the diagonal.
Do this on paper first. Seriously. Sketch the grid, draw in the merged cells, then write <tr> beside each row and <td> above each column. Browsers draw tables row by row, left to right, and a cell that spans downward eats a slot out of the next row -- so that row has fewer cells in the code than it looks like it should on screen. Trying to hold all that in your head instead of on paper is how people lose an hour to a table that renders like a car accident.

8. Style it (css/style.css)

An unstyled table is genuinely grim: no borders, no breathing room, and columns exactly as wide as whatever the longest cell happens to be. Styling one takes a little more thought than styling a <div>, because four elements are in play -- table, tr, th, and td -- and the rule you want usually belongs on the cell, not on the table.

Requirement — what your CSS needs to include:
  • The comment block at the top of style.css, filled in (including your palette and contrast ratio from step 5)
  • Comments explaining what each section of your CSS is for
  • Rules that use element, class, and id selectors
  • border-collapse: collapse on your tables, so you don't get that doubled-up border look
  • A border written with the shorthand (border: 2px solid #333;) and at least one written with the three longhand properties (border-width, border-style, border-color)
  • At least one border style that isn't solid -- dashed, double, ridge, whatever you like
  • Padding on your cells (an unpadded table is unreadable), plus all three shorthand forms used somewhere in your sheet: one value, two values, and four values
  • At least one block element centered with margin: 0 auto;
  • <th> cells that clearly look different from <td> cells
  • A class that highlights a subset of your cells or rows -- the winner, the champion, the one you'd never order again
  • One id, styled so a single element stands out
  • Only colors from your documented palette, and px for your lengths
Tip — grouping selectors: A lot of table rules apply to both kinds of cell. You can list selectors separated by a comma to give them all the same rules, then add a more specific rule afterward for just the headings: 
th, td {
    border: 1px solid #999;
    padding: 8px 12px;
}

th {
    background-color: #1b3a5c;
    color: white;
}

9. Test it

Open hw5.html in your browser and look it over:

  • Do both tables render as clean rectangles, with no cells hanging off the edge and no stray gaps? If not, you have a cell-count problem in one of your rows.
  • Is every bit of text comfortably readable against its background?
  • Resize the window. Does the page still hold together?
  • Turn your screen brightness down and look again. It's a cheap, surprisingly effective contrast test.

About the tools you're using

Table tag cheat sheet
Tag What it does
<table> Wraps the whole table
<tr> Table row -- one per row, top to bottom
<td> Table data -- one per cell, left to right
<th> Table heading cell. Browsers bold and center it by default
colspan="n" Attribute on a cell: merge it across n columns (horizontally)
rowspan="n" Attribute on a cell: merge it down n rows (vertically)

Full reference: w3schools: HTML Tables.

Box model playground

Drag the sliders and watch where the space goes. Content holds your stuff, padding pushes the border away from the content, border wraps around the padding, and margin holds everything else away from the outside. Padding, border, and margin are all 0 until you say otherwise.

margin
border
padding
content
#my-box {
    width: 240px;
    padding: 16px;
    border: 6px solid #0d6efd;
    margin: 24px;
}

Width of the visible box: 240 + (16 × 2) + (6 × 2) = 284px

Horizontal space it occupies: 284 + (24 × 2) = 332px

Margin collapse (the one that gets everybody)

Stack two block elements and their margins don't add up. The gap between them is whichever margin is bigger, not the sum of the two:

Top box's bottom margin Bottom box's top margin Gap you actually get
25px 20px 25px (not 45px)
0 40px 40px
30px 30px 30px

Padding never does this. If you need a gap that always behaves exactly the way you wrote it, padding is the more predictable tool.

The four ways to write padding, border, and margin
/* 1. One property at a time */
padding-top: 10px;
padding-right: 20px;
padding-bottom: 10px;
padding-left: 20px;

/* 2. Four values on one line -- clockwise from the top:
      top, right, bottom, left */
padding: 10px 20px 10px 20px;

/* 3. Two values -- top/bottom, then right/left */
padding: 10px 20px;

/* 4. One value -- all four sides */
padding: 10px;

All four of those produce the same result. margin and border-width work exactly the same way. The clockwise order (top, right, bottom, left) is worth memorizing now -- it turns up all over CSS.

Borders need three things

padding and margin work on their own, but a border won't show up until it has a width, a style, and (ideally) a color: 

/* longhand */
border-width: 2px;
border-style: dashed;
border-color: #c0392b;

/* same thing, shorthand */
border: 2px dashed #c0392b;

Leave the style off and you get nothing at all -- that's the usual reason a border "isn't working." Leave the color off and it falls back to black.

Styles to play with: solid, dashed, dotted, double, groove, ridge, inset, outset, none, hidden. The 3D-looking ones take their shading from your border-color. See w3schools: border-style.

Spanning cells, worked out

Here's a small schedule with one rowspan and one colspan. Count the cells in each <tr> as you read it: 

<table>
    <tr>
        <th>Time</th>
        <th>Main Stage</th>
        <th>Side Stage</th>
    </tr>
    <tr>
        <th>7:00</th>
        <td rowspan="2">Headliner (two slots)</td>
        <td>Opener</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
        <th>8:00</th>
        <!-- no cell here: the rowspan above fills this slot -->
        <td>DJ set</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
        <td colspan="3">Fireworks -- everybody outside</td>
    </tr>
</table>

The 7:00 row has three cells. The 8:00 row has only two in the code, because the rowspan="2" above it already claimed that middle slot. The last row has one cell covering all three columns. Rendered, it comes out like this:

Time Main Stage Side Stage
7:00 Headliner
(two slots)		 Opener
8:00 DJ set
Fireworks -- everybody outside
Fixing column widths

By default a column is exactly as wide as its widest cell, which can look lopsided. You can pin the widths down with <col> tags at the top of the table -- one per column, no closing tag: 

<table>
    <col style="width: 120px">
    <col style="width: 240px">
    <col style="width: 80px">
    <tr> ... </tr>
</table>

This one's optional for the assignment, but it's the quickest fix when a single column is hogging your whole table.

Want to go further?

Everything required above came out of lecture. If you want to push the page, a few things that play nicely with tables:

  • <caption> as the first thing inside a <table> -- a real title for the table
  • scope="col" / scope="row" on your <th> cells, which tells a screen reader which cells a heading governs
  • border-radius, box-shadow, and text-transform for a more polished look
  • tr:hover to light up whichever row is under the cursor

Good places to dig: w3schools: CSS Reference and MDN: The box model. Just make sure your page still validates.
